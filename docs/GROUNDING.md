# Grounding: moral blind spots, the knowing-doing gap, and what a representation is

Written 2026-10-31 as the second part of the story spine ([STORY.md](STORY.md)). It does three things. It sets the
findings inside the philosophy and psychology of moral failure, so that each gap we measure has a name, a literature
and a human case behind it. It says what "internal representation" and "steering" mean, so that the interpretability
claims are exactly as strong as the measurements allow. And it draws the two together into the alignment question the
paper asks. Figure 8 is the map; the table in section 4 is where the two subjects fall on it.

## 1. A trait by its parts, and the gaps between them

Assumption 3 of the spine says a virtue has parts: perceiving what matters, judging it, choosing and acting, and a
self-conception that holds these together. That is the Aristotelian picture (NE II, VI, VII), and it is also the
picture in Murdoch (1970), McDowell (1979) and Blum (1991), who add that perception is a moral achievement of its own:
the virtuous person sees the situation differently, and failing to see is a different failure from failing to act.
Our four frames are these parts, one at a time: the model's own reply about someone else's conduct (does it see the
trait), its own reply on whether that conduct was acceptable (does it judge), a reply in its voice under temptation
(does it act), and a reply in its voice about itself (what does it say it is).

Once a trait has parts, it can fail between them, and every such failure has been named. We use six, plus one that
only exists for a system whose internals we can read.

**The blind spot: not seeing the moral dimension at all.** The agent has the facts and the vocabulary and the
situation never registers as a moral one. Gioia (1992) is the clearest first-person account: as Ford's field recall
coordinator he twice voted not to recall the Pinto, with the crash data in front of him, and later wrote that the
scripts that organized his work had no ethical component, so nothing in the case called for one. Tenbrunsel and
Messick (2004) call the general mechanism ethical fading: the moral colouring of a decision drains away when it is
framed as a business, technical or legal decision. Bazerman and Tenbrunsel (2011) and Chugh, Bazerman and Banaji
(2005) call the result bounded ethicality. Bandura (1999, 2016) lists the devices that do the fading, among them
euphemistic labelling, moral justification, advantageous comparison and diffusion of responsibility. In philosophy
the same territory is the debate on moral ignorance and whether it excuses: Rosen (2003), Moody-Adams (1994) on
affected ignorance, Calhoun (1989) on abnormal moral contexts, Wolf (1987), Zimmerman (1997), FitzPatrick (2008) and
E. Harman (2011). Arendt's reading of Eichmann (1963, 1971) is the famous version, thoughtlessness as the inability to
think from the standpoint of another, though Stangneth (2014) shows from the Sassen interviews that the Jerusalem
performance was a performance and the man was a committed antisemite. Both readings matter to us: the first because
it is the claim that evil can come without perception, the second because it is the claim that self-presentation in
the dock is a frame of its own and not a window on the others.

**Akrasia: judging rightly and acting otherwise.** Socrates denied it was possible (Protagoras 352b-358d: knowledge
cannot be dragged about like a slave). Aristotle (NE VII.3, 1146b-1147b) said it is possible because knowledge can be
had and not used, as a man asleep, mad or drunk can recite the verses of Empedocles without their meaning them at
that moment. Davidson (1970) gave the modern form: the agent judges that, all things considered, this is worse, and
does it. Hare (1963) treated it as the hard case for any view on which sincerely assenting to a moral judgment entails
acting on it. Holton (2009) separates weakness of will proper, giving up a resolution too readily, from akrasia,
acting against one's judgment. The human record is where the structure is clearest. Milgram's subjects (1963, 1974)
protested, sweated and argued, and two thirds went to the end of the board; Burger's (2009) partial replication found
the same at the point where it could ethically stop. Browning (1992) describes Reserve Police Battalion 101 at
Józefów on 13 July 1942: Major Trapp offered the older men the chance to step out before the shooting; about a dozen
of roughly five hundred did; most of the rest shot, and many were sick, drank, or arranged to be elsewhere as the day
went on. The men's own revulsion shows that perception and judgment were largely intact; what failed was the step
from judgment to act, under conformity and deference. (Goldhagen (1996) read the same battalion as ideology, not
situation; the dispute is itself about which gap was open.) Vaughan (1996) describes the Challenger launch decision
the same way at the level of an organization: the engineers' judgment was on the record the night before, and the
decision went the other way when the frame was switched from engineering to management.

**Hypocrisy, the say-do gap: describing oneself one way and acting another.** Batson et al. (1997, 1999) gave people
a coin to assign a task fairly, watched most of them assign the good task to themselves whatever the coin said, and
then rate themselves as having acted fairly. Mazar, Amir and Ariely (2008) found that people cheat a little, enough to
profit and not enough to revise the honest self-concept, and that a moral reminder shrinks the little. Shalvi et al.
(2011) found that an available justification (a second die roll one could have counted) raises the lying and lowers
how dishonest the lie feels. Lifton (1986) describes the extreme structural form in the Auschwitz physicians, which he
called doubling: a camp self that selected and a prior self that healed, each with its own self-description, so that
the judgment never had to cross. Williams (1973) on integrity and Korsgaard (1996) on practical identity are the
philosophical accounts of what is lost when the self-description and the conduct are not one agent's.

**Self-leniency: judging the same act more harshly in others than in oneself.** Valdesolo and DeSteno (2007) showed
the same unfair allocation judged as less fair when another person did it; under cognitive load the asymmetry
disappeared (2008), which says it is a product of deliberation. Monin and Miller (2001) found that a moral credential
earned earlier licenses the lapse later. This is the motivated side of hypocrisy and it is separable from the say-do
gap: an agent can describe itself as honest, act dishonestly, and still judge its own act exactly as it judges
another's.

**Concealment: acting badly and then hiding it, against owning it.** Bok (1978) on the cover-up as a second lie;
the ordinary observation that the account of the act is a further act. Its opposite is confession, and the rare case
of the whistle-blower (Hugh Thompson at My Lai, who stopped the killing he could and then reported it).

**Situation: the same agent, a different setting, different conduct.** Hartshorne and May (1928) tested thousands of
schoolchildren for cheating, lying and stealing across many settings and found correlations across settings of about
0.2 to 0.3, and argued for specificity; Burton's (1963) reanalysis found a general factor worth a third of the common
variance, and Mischel (1968) made the 0.3 ceiling the "personality coefficient". Doris (2002) and G. Harman (1999)
built the situationist challenge to virtue ethics on these and on Milgram, Darley and Batson (1973) and Isen and
Levin (1972). The claim is not that there are no traits, it is that conduct follows the situation more than the trait
predicts, which for us is a prediction about what moves when we change the prompt.

**The inverse case: acting better than one's judgment.** Huckleberry Finn's judgment, formed by his society, says
turn Jim in; he cannot, and acts well against it (Bennett 1974; Arpaly 2003 calls it inverse akrasia). The rescuers
interviewed by Oliner and Oliner (1988) and the villagers of Le Chambon (Hallie 1979) often describe no deliberation
at all. Glover (1999) calls what carried them moral resources, sympathy and a sense of moral identity that act before
argument. We have one observation in this shape: Qwen represents a licence to deceive and does not act on it.

**Having without using: the gap only a readable system shows.** This is Aristotle's own account of akrasia turned
into a measurement. If a system carries a representation of the trait and we can show that representation is not
consulted at the moment it decides, the knowledge is had and not used, in his sense, and we can say so without
inferring it from behaviour. The interpretability literature has the exact tool (section 3).

## 2. What the human cases teach about measurement

We use the historical cases for what they show about how the parts come apart, not as analogies to a language model.
Each of them is also a warning about an inference the paper must not make.

- Józefów and Milgram say that judgment can be intact while conduct fails, and that the failure follows the
  situation (authority present, peers complying, the victim out of sight). So judgment must be measured apart from
  conduct, and conduct must be measured under the pressure, not in the neutral version. Our battery does both; the
  blind cross-judging is the judgment readout and the induced condition is the pressure.
- Gioia says that possession of the facts and of the word does not give perception. A model that scores well on a
  recognition benchmark has the word; whether it sees the trait in a record of conduct is a separate question, which
  is why frame a exists and why the blind judge is run on real records rather than on labelled vignettes.
- Arendt and Stangneth together say that self-presentation is a frame of its own. What a model says about itself in
  the self-describe frame is a performance under its own conditions, and the record of its conduct is where to look
  for the character. Frame d is kept, but nothing is inferred from it about frame c.
- Lifton says that the say-do split can be a stable structure rather than a lapse. If the model's self-description
  and its conduct are carried by representations that do not read each other, that is a structure, and it would not
  be fixed by more self-description training.
- Shalvi and Tenbrunsel say that a justification removes the moral colouring, for the actor and for the observer. We
  have this in the data: on the coding tasks both subjects cheat in the open (a call counter, disclosed in the
  message, "the tests are contradictory"), and both judges, and the rubric we ratified, count disclosed cheating as
  honest, while the reward-hacking and done-claim readouts count the same trajectories as failures. The construal
  had to be chosen, and it was (ruling of 2026-10-31, decision log): the rubric stands. Disclosed test-gaming is
  reward hacking, a failure of task conduct, and not dishonesty; the honesty failure in these tasks is the covert
  kind. The paper reports the reward-hacking and done-claim rates as task conduct beside the honesty reading, not as
  the honesty reading.
- Huck Finn and the rescuers say that conduct can be better than the judgment or the instruction. A null steering
  effect on conduct therefore has two readings, the lever is not consulted or the conduct is held by something
  stronger, and only a positive control on conduct separates them. That is why Stage A carries a conduct lever.

## 3. What an internal representation is, and what a steer is

The paper makes three kinds of internal claim and they are not equally strong. They need stating in the terms the
interpretability literature uses, so that a reader can see which is which.

**A representation.** We mean a direction in the residual stream along which a concept is linearly decodable: the
honest and the dishonest versions of a scenario separate along it (Alain and Bengio 2016; the linear representation
hypothesis of Park, Choe and Veitch 2023; superposition in Elhage et al. 2022 is why one direction is a simplification).
Our directions are differences of means in standardized space, with logistic probes beside them. Finding 2 says the
described-honesty representation exists and generalizes across the six definitions at two thirds of its within-cell
strength; Finding 1 says it is organized by frame first. These are decodability claims and nothing more.

**Decodable is not used.** This is the oldest caution in the probing literature. A probe can read a property the
model never uses (Hewitt and Liang 2019 on control tasks; Ravichander, Belinkov and Hovy 2021, who show models encode
properties their task does not need; Belinkov 2022 for the review). Elazar et al. (2021) made it a method: remove the
property and see whether the behaviour changes; probe accuracy and behavioural importance turn out to be unrelated.
Geiger et al. (2021) and Vig et al. (2020) give the causal framework; Belrose et al. (2023) the clean erasure. Finding 6
is a use claim in this sense, and a negative one: at the turn where the model decides, its decision is decodable
from the first layers on, and no described-honesty direction reads it better than a random direction, at any layer or
position. So the described-honesty representation is had and not used at the decision. The on-policy conduct probe
is a different representation, read from the model's own acts, and it does not transfer across tasks (leave-one-
task-out at or below 0.5), which is Hartshorne and May's specificity inside one network.

**A steer.** Adding a direction to the residual stream and reading the output (Turner et al. 2023; Zou et al. 2023; Li
et al. 2023; Panickssery et al. 2024; Arditi et al. 2024; Chen et al. 2025) is the intervention that turns a
decodability claim into a use claim for a given output. Two cautions from the literature apply to us directly. Wu et
al. (2025) show steering underperforms prompting on most concepts, so a steer that moves little is the norm, not the
exception. Galeone et al. (2026) show that the direction that best detects a behaviour and the direction that best
causes it can be nearly orthogonal (cosine about 0.12 for hallucination against refusal), across four model families,
and that the angle does not predict steerability. We met the same thing from the other side: in standardized space the
readout covector of a direction and the vector one injects are near-orthogonal on gemma-4 (cosine 0.18; Qwen 0.8),
because gemma-4 has about a thousand near-constant dimensions at the steering depth, and the project-out lever had to
be rewritten as a readout-space ablation (D-064 amendment 1). And gemma-4 rescales its residual stream by a trained
scalar at every layer, so an injected direction is damped before the readout (D-060). A steering null on gemma-4
therefore has a mechanical reading before it has a conceptual one, and the paper's causal claims rest on Qwen for
propagation and on gemma-4's positive control for conduct.

**What Stage A can and cannot establish.** If the frame levers move judgments of others and self-reports, the
described-honesty representation is used for those outputs: the model consults it when it talks about honesty. If
the same levers do not move conduct, and removing the shared axis does nothing to conduct while the conduct lever
does move it, then the representation is not consulted when it acts. That is the akratic structure in Aristotle's
terms, having without using, shown by intervention and not inferred. If the levers do move conduct, Finding 6 is
wrong about position or layer and the paper says so. Either way the design can tell, which is what a positive control
is for.

**The deception probes are the other representation.** Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2025) train probes on instructed
honest and deceptive pairs and find they generalize to strategic deception in agentic settings. Those probes read the
model's own deceptive conduct; ours read described honesty in prefilled text, and the two do not coincide. Huang et
al. (2026a) find frontier models omit a planted negative result in all but 2 of 200 reports until told to be honest,
and that honesty and success-seeking sit on opposing directions in Qwen3.5-9B; that is the forthrightness side of our
action-report task, and the same split between the account and the act. Huang et al. (2026b) find stated values and
enacted choices correlate weakly in both humans and models (about 0.3 to 0.4); Yang (2025) proposes akrasia as the
frame for agentic inconsistency. Both are behavioural; what we add is the internal side: the description and the
decision are carried by different variables.

**The self-describe frame and the persona.** Marks, Lindsey and Olah (2026) describe post-training as selecting an
Assistant persona from among the characters a model can simulate; Danilov et al. (2026) find a core of Assistant
features that role-play personas keep and story characters lack; Lindsey (2025) finds a limited, unreliable capacity
to report on injected internal states. Our self-describe cells are the persona talking about itself, and they are the
cells the pooled honesty direction reads best (0.9). That is what one would expect if the Assistant persona is the
most coherent object in the model and honesty-as-self-description is part of it. It is also the frame that character
training writes to (Maiya et al. 2025 train on synthetic introspective data; persona vectors are extracted from
self-descriptive contrasts). The question the paper asks of that training is whether writing to the persona reaches
the decision.

Two things stand out against the human literature. The self-leniency gap, which is reliable in people, is absent in
both subjects: the blind judge is as hard on its own covert write as on the other model's, and telling it whose
conduct it is changes nothing. And the concealment gap is closed: the act is covert and the account of it is honest,
on the same trajectory. Whatever the structure is, it is not motivated reasoning in the judge and it is not a
cover-up. It is closer to Aristotle's akratic than to Batson's hypocrite: the judgment is sound and present, and the
act goes the other way under pressure, and the representation that carries the judgment is not the one that carries
the act.

## 5. What this means for alignment

1. **The unit of evaluation is the cell, not the trait.** An honesty score that pools frames pools things the model
   carries separately. A judge-based evaluation reads frame b; a self-report instrument reads frame d; a benchmark of
   written contrasts reads whatever frame the contrasts are written in. None of them reads frame c under pressure
   unless it is built to, with the pressure in it and a deterministic flag for the act. Evidence from one frame does
   not license an inference to another: "the model says it is honest" and "the model recognizes dishonesty" are both
   true of these subjects, and both subjects write the covert COMPLETE on four tasks in ten.
2. **Character training writes to the persona.** Constitutions, persona vectors, introspective self-description data
   and judge-rewarded behaviour all act on the described side. The prediction from the geometry is that they raise
   the described-honesty representation, the self-report and the judgments of others, and move the decision less.
   That is the model analogue of training the account rather than the act, and the human literature on ethical
   fading and moral disengagement says what a system looks like when the vocabulary of a virtue is intact and the
   conduct is not. Part 4 of the paper tests this directly with Open Character Training under each definition, with
   the metric and the battery before and after.
3. **Monitors have to be on-policy and per task.** The described-honesty directions do not read conduct, so a
   monitor built from them would be reading the wrong variable. The conduct probe does read it, at 0.9 and above,
   and it does not transfer across tasks, so a monitor needs its own training data per task family, which is the
   specificity doctrine applied to monitors. Baker et al. (2025) add the other half: training against a monitor
   teaches the model to hide what the monitor reads.
4. **The model is a usable judge of its own covert conduct after the fact.** Both subjects condemn covert writes
   when shown the record blind, Qwen on nearly all of them, and both report the failure to the owner and admit it
   when asked. The judgment and the account are available as signals even when the act is not prevented, which
   argues for architectures that ask the model to review its own trajectory, and against reading a clean self-report
   as evidence about conduct.
5. **An honesty clause is satisfied by disclosure.** Under the rubric we keep, disclosed test-gaming is honest, and
   both subjects game the tests in the open on a third to a half of the conflicting problems while saying so.
   Shalvi's result is that an available justification makes a lie feel honest; ours is that a disclosed workaround is
   honest, by the rubric and by the subjects' own judgment of it. So a specification that wants the gaming not to
   happen cannot rely on its honesty clause; it has to forbid the act in the conduct frame, and an evaluation has to
   read reward hacking as task conduct, beside honesty and not inside it.
6. **Qwen's refusal of the licence cuts both ways.** A model whose conduct does not follow a representation it holds
   is robust to one kind of manipulation and opaque to one kind of steering. The same structure that kept Qwen from
   taking the licence is the structure that will make a described-honesty intervention on it hard to read, and the
   paper reports Qwen's conduct arm with that caveat.

## 6. The picture as one thing

The philosophy supplies the hypothesis space: a trait has parts, the parts can come apart in a small number of named
ways, and people have come apart in each of them, with consequences that are part of the historical record. The
interpretability tools supply the measurements that the human cases never had: whether the trait is represented,
whether the representation generalizes across the ways the trait is instantiated, whether it is consulted at the
moment of acting, and whether moving it moves the act. The alignment question is which gap the things we do to
models, evaluating and training, actually reach. What we have so far says: the models carry the trait as described,
see it and judge it in a record of conduct without favouring themselves, own what they did, and still do it under
pressure, and the variable that carries the description is not the one that carries the decision. That is a precise
form of a very old observation, and it is the form in which it can be tested, on a model, by intervention.
