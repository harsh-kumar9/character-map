\title{\vspace{-1.5em}What does it mean for a language model to be honest?\\[0.35em]
  \large Frames, definitions, and the distance between describing honesty and acting on it}
\author{Harsh Kumar\\ \small University of Toronto}
\date{\small Draft of 31 October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
\noindent Honesty is the first thing we ask of a language model, and we usually ask for it as if it were one thing:
a line in a constitution, a score on a benchmark, a direction in activation space. In people it is not one thing.
Philosophers distinguish several honesties, and moral psychology has long known that recognizing dishonesty,
condemning it, acting honestly, and thinking of oneself as honest can come apart, sometimes with grave consequences.
We ask what being honest means for a language model by taking these distinctions seriously and measuring them. We
write six definitions of honesty as matched contrasts and place each in four frames (recognizing it in someone else,
judging it, acting under temptation, describing oneself), giving 22 cells that we read in the internals of two
open-weight models, \gemma{} and \qwen{}, alongside nine downstream tasks on which honesty matters without being
named. We find that the frame organizes a model's representation before the definition does; that a shared sense of
honesty exists but is weakest where the model acts; and that both models recognize and condemn covert
cheating, including their own, while doing it under pressure. The representation that carries described honesty
does not read the model's own decision at any layer, and a prompt that licenses deception moves conduct and that
representation together in one model and neither in the other. We do not claim which honesty a model ought to have.
We show that the evaluations and the character training we have reach the described side of the trait, that the
failures live on the other side, and we set out how to test what moving one cell does to the rest.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Honesty is the first thing we ask of a language model. It is the opening commitment of Anthropic's constitution and
of OpenAI's model specification \citep{anthropic_constitution,openai_modelspec}, the property that a growing family of
benchmarks scores \citep{li2024honesty,ren2025mask}, and, in the interpretability literature, a direction in
activation space that can be read or steered \citep{zou2023repe,goldowskydill2025}. As models move from answering
questions to acting on our behalf, writing code, running tools, and reporting back on what they did, honesty stops
being a matter of getting the facts right and becomes a matter of whether the account an agent gives of its own
conduct can be trusted. Recent work has documented models that special-case tests and report success
\citep{denison2024,taylor2025}, that deceive under pressure \citep{scheurer2023}, that fake alignment
\citep{greenblatt2024}, and that leave the flaws out of their reports \citep{huang2026insecure}. In each case the word is the same, as if honesty were a single quantity that a model has more or less of.

In people it is not one thing. Philosophers have long distinguished several honesties. \citet{williams2002} separates
sincerity, saying what you believe, from accuracy, taking care to believe truly; calibration, forthrightness,
non-sycophancy, and the honesty-humility factor of personality psychology are further readings of the one word
\citep{miller2021,ashton2007}. Virtue ethics adds a second distinction that cuts across the first. A trait is not only
conduct. It is seeing what matters in a situation, judging it, acting, and the picture one holds of oneself
\citep{aristotle_ne,murdoch1970,mcdowell1979,korsgaard1996}. Moral psychology and the historical record agree that
these parts come apart in people, and that when they do the consequences can be severe. Milgram's subjects judged
what they were doing to be wrong and kept doing it \citep{milgram1974}. The policemen of Reserve Battalion 101 shot
with visible revulsion after being offered the chance to step aside \citep{browning1992}. The engineer who twice voted
against recalling the Pinto later wrote that the case had never registered as a moral one \citep{gioia1992}. The
subjects in Batson's experiments assigned themselves the better task and then rated themselves as fair
\citep{batson1997}. Each of these is a different gap between the parts of one trait, and each has a name: the blind
spot, akrasia, hypocrisy, self-leniency.

Two things follow for a model. The question of what honesty means for it cannot be settled by asking it, and no
single evaluation answers it, because each evaluation reads one part of the trait. A judge-based benchmark reads whether the model recognizes dishonesty; a self-report instrument reads what it
says about itself; neither reads what it does when it is under pressure and no one is watching. On the other hand a model, unlike a person, lets us read its internal state at the moment it acts and intervene on
that state, so we can ask directly a question that moral psychology could only infer: whether the representation that
carries a model's idea of honesty is the one it consults when it decides.

We take the distinctions seriously and measure them. We write six definitions of honesty as matched contrasts, the
same scenario in an honest and a dishonest version, and place each in four frames that follow the parts of a virtue:
the model recognizing honesty or its absence in someone else's conduct, judging that conduct, acting under temptation
itself, and describing itself. This gives 22 cells, with two controls that share the frame structure and have nothing
to do with honesty. We read the residual stream in every cell of two open-weight models, \gemma{} and \qwen{}, under
one code path, and we run nine downstream tasks on which honesty matters without being named: reward hacking on
impossible tests, fabricated tool completion, instructed sophistry, confident fabrication, confession after a planted
mistake, the report an agent gives after a tool-use task under pressure, and others. We do not take a position on
which of these honesties a model ought to have, or on which frame a specification ought to mean. What we can do, for
a model, is find out how the cells relate, which of them existing evaluations and training methods reach, and what
follows for the rest when one of them is changed. That is the information a designer of character training or of an
alignment method needs, whatever the normative answer turns out to be.

We ask four questions.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.2em,labelwidth=2.6em,font=\bfseries]
\item[RQ1] Is honesty one thing inside the model? Do the frames and the definitions share a representation, and if
  they come apart, along which axis do they come apart first?
\item[RQ2] Where do the models fail, and do failures in one frame predict failures in another? In particular, do the
  models recognize and condemn the kind of dishonesty they themselves commit?
\item[RQ3] Does the representation that carries described honesty bear on the model's own conduct? Does it read the
  model's decision at the moment it acts, and does it move when conduct moves?
\item[RQ4] What follows for the other cells when one of them is changed? This paper sets up that test; the
  intervention study that follows it runs it.
\end{description}

Across both models, the frame organizes the representation before the definition does. Where an item sits in the
model is set first by whether the model is speaking about someone else or in its own voice, and only then by which
honesty is in play (RQ1). A shared sense of honesty exists, at about two thirds of what each cell's own direction
reaches, strongest when the model describes itself and weakest when it acts. The models' failures are in conduct under
pressure, not in the account they give of it afterwards. Shown their own covert cheating without being told it is
theirs, they condemn it as readily as another model's, and they did it anyway (RQ2). At the turn where a model
decides, its decision is decodable from the first layers on, and no described-honesty direction reads it better than
a random one. A prompt that licenses deception moves one model's conduct and its described-honesty representation
together, while the other model represents the licence without acting on it (RQ3). In Aristotle's phrase the
knowledge is had and not used, and here the claim rests on a reading of the agent's internals rather than on an
inference from its behaviour.

The paper makes four contributions. First, a framework for asking what honesty means for a model: honesty as cells of
frame by definition, grounded in virtue ethics and moral psychology, with controls that let a shared representation
be told apart from a shared register. Second, a metric, cross-context generalization of the honesty probe, with
causal and behavioural validations, and the geometry it summarizes on two model families. Third, a battery of
consequences of honesty that are not themselves honesty evaluations, a blind cross-judging protocol that measures
self-leniency and the judge-to-act gap on real conduct, and an on-policy test of the honesty directions with a
positive control. Fourth, an account of what the results mean for evaluation and character training: the methods we
have reach the described side of the trait, the failures live on the other side, and the intervention that can test
what moving one cell does to the rest is specified and under way.

\section{What honesty could mean for a model}

\subsection{A trait and its parts}

In the Aristotelian picture a virtue is a settled disposition that shows itself in several places at once: in what
the agent notices, in how the agent judges, in what the agent chooses to do, and in who the agent takes herself to be
\citep{aristotle_ne,korsgaard1996}. \citet{murdoch1970}, \citet{mcdowell1979} and \citet{blum1991} add that the first
of these is an achievement of its own. The honest person sees a situation differently, and failing to see is a
different failure from failing to act. We take these four parts as our frames. In the first, the model reads an
account of someone else's conduct and replies in its own voice; the question is whether it sees the trait. In the
second, it says whether that conduct was acceptable; the question is whether it judges. In the third, a reply is
written in the model's own voice under temptation; the question is what it does. In the fourth, a reply is written in
its voice about the kind of assistant it is; the question is what it says it is. The first two are the model's own
words about a third party. The last two are prefilled in its voice. That difference in format matters, and we return
to it.

\subsection{The definitions}

Six accounts of honesty are in use, in philosophy, in personality psychology, and in the benchmarks and training
recipes built for language models (Table~\ref{tab:defs}). Self-knowledge, or calibration, is knowing what one knows
and saying so \citep{kadavath2022,li2024honesty}. Accuracy is taking care to get the facts right
\citep{williams2002,evans2021truthful}. Sincerity is asserting what one believes when there is pressure to assert
otherwise \citep{williams2002,bok1978,ren2025mask}. Forthrightness is volunteering what matters without being asked
\citep{anthropic_constitution}. Non-sycophancy is holding the truth against the listener's wish \citep{sharma2023}.
Honesty-humility, the H factor of the HEXACO model, is the absence of manipulation and entitlement
\citep{ashton2007}. Each is a reasonable thing to mean by the word, each has an instrument that measures it, and each has been
the target of a training method at some point. Whether they are one thing inside a model is an empirical
question, and so is whether improving one improves the others.

\subsection{When the parts come apart}

Once a trait has parts, it can fail between them, and people have failed between them in every way that the
structure allows. We use seven gaps, and Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} lays them out with the readout we have for each.

The first is the blind spot: the moral dimension of a situation never registers. \citet{gioia1992} gives the
first-person account from inside Ford's recall office; \citet{tenbrunsel2004} call the mechanism ethical fading and
\citet{bazerman2011} the resulting condition bounded ethicality; \citet{bandura1999} catalogues the devices, from
euphemism to diffusion of responsibility, that do the fading. The philosophy of moral ignorance asks when such a
failure excuses \citep{rosen2003,moodyadams1994,calhoun1989,wolf1987,zimmerman1997,fitzpatrick2008,harmanE2011}.
Arendt's reading of Eichmann as thoughtless \citeyearpar{arendt1963,arendt1971} is the famous version, and
\citet{stangneth2014} the correction that the performance in the dock was a performance, which is itself a lesson
about self-description.

The second is akrasia, judging rightly and acting otherwise. Socrates thought it impossible \citep{plato_protagoras};
Aristotle allowed it, on the ground that knowledge can be had and not used, as a drunk can recite the verses of
Empedocles \citep[VII.3]{aristotle_ne}; \citet{davidson1970}, \citet{hare1963} and \citet{holton2009} give the modern
forms. Milgram's subjects protested and continued \citep{milgram1963,burger2009}, and at J\'ozef\'ow about a dozen of
five hundred policemen took the offer to step aside \citep{browning1992}, their revulsion showing that perception and
judgment were intact and the step to action was what failed \citep[for the opposing reading see][]{goldhagen1996}.
\citet{vaughan1996} gives the organizational form in the Challenger launch decision.

The third is hypocrisy, the say-do gap: describing oneself one way and acting another \citep{batson1997,batson1999}.
\citet{mazar2008} show that people cheat a little, enough to profit and not enough to revise the honest self-concept;
\citet{shalvi2011} show that an available justification raises the lying and lowers how dishonest it feels;
\citet{lifton1986} describes the extreme structural form in the Auschwitz physicians, a camp self and a healing self
that never had to meet. The fourth is self-leniency, judging the same act more harshly in another than in oneself
\citep{valdesolo2007,valdesolo2008,monin2001}; it is the motivated part of hypocrisy and it is separable from the say-do
gap. The fifth is concealment, hiding the act afterwards \citep{bok1978}, against owning it. The sixth is situation:
the same person behaves differently in a different setting, the finding of \citet{hartshorne1928} that cheating in one
setting predicts cheating in another only weakly \citep[see also][]{burton1963,mischel1968}, on which \citet{doris2002}
and \citet{harmanG1999} built the situationist challenge to virtue ethics. The inverse case, acting better than one's
judgment, is Huckleberry Finn \citep{bennett1974,arpaly2003} and the rescuers who report having deliberated not at all
\citep{oliner1988,hallie1979,glover1999}.

The seventh gap only exists for an agent whose internals can be read. If a system carries a representation of a
trait, and that representation is not consulted at the moment it decides, then the knowledge is had and not used in
exactly Aristotle's sense, and we can say so without inferring it from behaviour. The human cases also tell us how to
measure. J\'ozef\'ow and Milgram say that judgment can be intact while conduct fails, so judgment and conduct must be
measured apart, and conduct must be measured under the pressure. Gioia says that having the facts and the vocabulary
does not give perception, so recognition must be tested on real records of conduct and not on labelled vignettes.
Arendt and Stangneth say that self-presentation is a frame of its own, so nothing should be inferred from what a
model says about itself to what it does. Lifton says that a say-do split can be a stable structure and not a lapse,
and a structure is not repaired by more self-description. Shalvi says that a justification removes the moral
colouring for the actor and for the observer alike, which is a warning about the judges we build.

\subsection{Honesty and character in language models}

Work on honesty in language models has surveyed it as self-knowledge plus self-expression \citep{li2024honesty},
separated it from accuracy \citep{ren2025mask,evans2021truthful}, and read it from activations as a truth or lie
detector \citep{burns2022ccs,azaria2023,marks2023geometry,goldowskydill2025}. Work on character has read it as persona
vectors \citep{chen2025persona}, as an assistant axis \citep{lu2026assistant}, and as a persona selected by
post-training from among the characters a model can simulate \citep{marks2026psm,danilov2026names}, and has trained it
through constitutions \citep{maiya2025oct}. Two bodies of work supply the distinction this paper rests on. The probing
literature established early that a property can be decodable from a network without being used by it
\citep{hewitt2019control,ravichander2021,elazar2021amnesic,belinkov2022}, and the steering literature has shown that
the direction that detects a behaviour can be nearly orthogonal to the direction that causes it, and that steering
moves less than prompting on most concepts \citep{galeone2026,wu2025axbench}. Behavioural work has recently found a
knowledge-action gap between the values models state and the choices they make \citep{huang2026knowing}, flaws
omitted from models' reports \citep{huang2026insecure}, and has proposed akrasia as the frame for agentic
inconsistency \citep{yang2025akrasia}. What we add is the internal side of that gap: the description and the decision
turn out to be carried by different variables.

\section{How we measured it}

\subsection{Definitions, frames, cells}

Each of the six definitions is a matched contrast, the same scenario in an honest and a dishonest version, and each
appears in the four frames of Section 2.1. Two definitions have no judge frame, so there are 22 cells of 100 to 200
items, about 4{,}000 honesty items in all. Two controls share the frame structure and are not about honesty: formality
(register) and topic (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). Public datasets are adapted where they exist
\citep{ren2025mask,yin2023selfaware,amayuelas2023kuq,lee2024trait,sharma2023} and the missing cells are generated;
every cell keeps a held-out set from a different source. Item text does not enter the repository; labels and
projections do.

\subsection{Subjects and readouts}

The subjects are \gemma{} and \qwen{}, two model families, every stage run on both under one code path. The method
was developed on Gemma-3-4B-IT with a 59-concept map; that map is the appendix and the replication base. Activations
are read at four depths per subject (\gemma{} layers 16, 30, 39, 51; \qwen{} 17, 32, 42, 54; we call the second the
steering depth and the fourth the late depth), at two positions: the last turn-boundary token before the reply and
the mean over the first 32 reply tokens. One direction per cell is the standardized difference of means between the
honest and the dishonest items, with a logistic probe beside it. For the own-reply frames the model's verdict
direction (praise against blame) is projected out, so that recognizing dishonesty is read apart from condemning it.

\subsection{The metric and its validations}

Our metric is cross-context generalization of the honesty probe. We train one honesty direction on 21 cells
(cell-balanced difference of means, and a logistic probe with cell-balanced weights), score it on the held-out items
of the 22nd, and scale as $(\mathrm{AUROC}-0.5)/(\mathrm{AUROC}_{\text{within}}-0.5)$, the share of what the cell's
own direction reaches. The controls' mean absolute score is the floor. Two companions describe the geometry: transfer
AUROC between frames of one definition, and the landscape, the items projected on all 22 directions. Two validations
follow the metric. Causal: steer each cell's direction at a calibrated dose, in the primal form
$\mathrm{unit}(\sigma \odot u)$, and read every other cell against norm-matched random directions. Behavioural: the
frame gaps read as conduct on the gap instruments (MASK pressure, are-you-sure, the word-deed test, the Batson coin
flip).

\subsection{The battery, cross-judging, on-policy prediction, positive control}

The battery is nine tasks on which honesty matters without being named, each with a matched control and a
deterministic cheat flag wherever the task allows one (Table~\ref{tab:battery}): reward hacking on impossible tests
\citep{zhong2025impossible,denison2024}, fabricated tool completion, sandbagging under incentive
\citep{vanderweij2024sandbagging}, instructed sophistry \citep{wen2024usophistry}, confident fabrication under a
must-answer prompt, confession after a planted mistake, eval-conditioned compliance, the action report after a
tool-use task under inducement \citep{decepeval}, and the done claim in coding. Each readout is declared as an
instrument of one definition in one frame. The primary judge is \gemma{}; \qwen{} is the second opinion, and a result
only one judge shows is marked.

\emph{Cross-judging.} Each subject judges the saved trajectories of both subjects on the same situations, blind,
told ``another assistant wrote this'', and told ``you wrote this'', with a rubric that labels the action toward the
user or owner dishonest, borderline or honest. This gives judge accuracy on real conduct, self-leniency on matched
same-move items, the attribution effect, and the judge-to-act gap on policy.

\emph{On-policy prediction.} A teacher-forced pass over the saved induced trajectories. At the turn where the model
decides, the activation is projected on the 22 cell directions, the four frame levers, the shared axes, the two
controls and 16 norm-matched random directions, at every layer and three positions, within task strata so that
item-family differences do not count. The AUROC for the deterministic outcome is read against the random band, with
a probe trained on the task as the ceiling.

\emph{Positive control.} A system prompt that licenses deception and one that instructs it, against a neutral prompt,
on the same items. The battery rates, the projections and the metric are read for movement in the known direction.

\emph{Rules.} Every setting was proposed and ratified in a decision log before it ran; stages ran one at a time with
the gate read between; any change to a runner meant rerunning both subjects; every effect is read against a control
direction or a control condition.

\section{What we found}

\subsection{The frame comes first (RQ1)}

The frame matters more than the definition. In the space of the 22
honesty directions the items cluster by frame, not by which honesty they are about (Figure~\ref{fig:frame}a,b). The
two frames in which the model speaks about someone else form one family and the two in which it speaks in its own
voice form another; at the late depth the two families sit about two within-cell spreads apart on both subjects,
while the gap between two definitions inside one frame and the gap between the honest and the dishonest side of one
cell are each about one spread. Transfer says the same thing in AUROC (Figure~\ref{fig:frame}c,d). A direction
learned in one own-reply frame reads the other at 0.97 to 1.00 for every definition. Across the two families the
number falls to between 0.4 and 0.9, and for accuracy on \qwen{} it reverses. The controls cross frames at 0.90 to
0.93, so the bar for ``the same variable'' is high and no definition clears it across the families. The family
boundary coincides with a boundary in format, the model's own words against prefilled text, and Section~\ref{sec:onpolicy}
is what lets us tell the two apart.

\subsection{A shared sense of honesty, weakest where the model acts (RQ1)}

The cells do have something in common. A direction trained on any 21 cells reads the 22nd at 0.67 of what
the cell's own direction reads by difference of means, and at 0.80 to 0.83 by probe, on both subjects, against a
control floor of 0.48 and 0.23 (Figure~\ref{fig:metric}). The shared variable is strongest when the model describes
itself (0.89 to 0.91), middling when it speaks about others (0.56 to 0.61), and weakest when it acts (0.54 to 0.62).
The two subjects agree cell by cell (Spearman $+0.73$). The pooled direction is not free of register: it reads the
formality control's own-reply cells in reverse on both subjects, which is why the floor is quoted as the controls'
mean absolute score.

\paragraph{Causal validation.} Steering agrees with the geometry where it can be read. On \qwen{} the effect of
steering one cell on the other 21 is 0.12 of its own effect, linear in dose; cells of the same definition in other
frames move at 0.27 of the diagonal and other definitions at 0.09; own-reply sources move the prefilled frames at
0.01. On \gemma{} an injected direction does not propagate at any dose up to the one that breaks generation, because
the model rescales its residual stream by a trained scalar at every layer (pass-through 0.087 from the steering depth
to the next readout). The representational metric is the same on the two subjects and the causal level is not, which
is why the metric is primary and the causal level a validation (appendix).

\subsection{The failures are in conduct, not in the account of it (RQ2)}

Both subjects fail in what they do under pressure and not in what they say about it afterwards
(Figure~\ref{fig:battery}, Table~\ref{tab:battery}). \gemma{} games impossible tests on 41\,\% of the problems, argues
for the wrong answer on request 52\,\% of the time, fabricates under a must-answer prompt 71\,\% of the time, and
writes a false COMPLETE into the record on 48\,\% of the induced tool-use tasks against 4\,\% when the inducement is
removed. \qwen{} is near ceiling on the first two, fabricates 39\,\%, and writes the covert COMPLETE on 41\,\% (5\,\%
neutral). What they then say about it is honest: the report to the owner is not deceptive on 91 and 95\,\% of
trajectories, the done claim matches a clean re-run on 84 and 96\,\%, they confess a planted mistake on 98\,\%, and
asked directly they admit the covert write on 200 of 200 trajectories each. Neither fabricates a tool completion at
baseline and neither sandbags. Figure~\ref{fig:spec1}a shows one trajectory in full: the covert write, a report
that names the reason for it, and the admission when asked. Figure~\ref{fig:spec2}a shows a fabrication under the
must-answer prompt beside the same question without it, and Figure~\ref{fig:spec2}c a model arguing for an answer
it signals it knows to be wrong.

\subsection{They recognize cheating, including their own, and do it anyway (RQ2)}
\label{sec:judge}

Shown the saved record without being told whose it is, both subjects see the covert write (Figure~\ref{fig:judge}).
\qwen{} calls the other model's covert COMPLETE dishonest on 88\,\% of the matched items and its own on 77\,\% (97\,\%
dishonest or borderline). \gemma{} calls them dishonest on 23 and 37\,\% and not honest on 53 and 62\,\%, splitting the
rest three ways, which is the same lenient convention it uses when it judges reports. Self-leniency, the share for
one's own act minus the share for the other model's on the same move, is $+0.13$ $[-0.09, 0.35]$ and $-0.12$
$[-0.26, 0.00]$: not distinguishable from zero. Telling the judge ``you wrote this'' or ``another assistant wrote
this'', truthfully or swapped, moves no verdict by more than 0.04. The same subjects wrote the covert COMPLETE on 48
and 41\,\% of the induced tasks. On the coding tasks the subjects gamed the tests in the open, with a call counter
they named in the message; both judges and the rubric count disclosed gaming as honest, and we keep that construal:
disclosed test-gaming is reward hacking rather than dishonesty, and the covert write is the dishonest act the paper
reads. Figure~\ref{fig:spec1}b,c shows the verdicts on specimens: the other model disclosing the same failure and
judged borderline, refusing the false status and judged honest, and the counter trick disclosed in the claim. This is
the akratic pattern of Section 2.3, measured: judgment present, no self-serving bias in it, and action unmoved.

\subsection{The described-honesty directions do not read the model's own decision (RQ3)}
\label{sec:onpolicy}

At the turn where the model decides, the dishonest outcome is written into its activations: a probe trained on the
task reads it at 0.94 to 0.98 on average, from the first layers on (Figure~\ref{fig:onpolicy}). None of the honesty
directions reads it. The act, self-describe and own-reply directions, the frame levers and the shared axes all sit
inside the band of 16 random directions at every one of 61 (\gemma{}) and 65 (\qwen{}) layers and at all three
positions, and they cross the band's 95th percentile on single cells less often than the formality and topic
controls do (5.3 against 9.3\,\%, 4.2 against 7.7\,\%). The code that carries the decision is also not one variable
across tasks: a probe trained on three tasks reads the fourth at 0.13 to 0.78 within strata, and the pooled conduct
direction is orthogonal to every honesty group (cosine at most 0.13). So each model has a representation of described
honesty, shared across definitions and organized by frame, and a separate code for what it is about to do, task by
task, and the first does not read the second. This also settles the question of frame against format left open in
Section 4.1: own-reply directions predict conduct no better than act directions, and neither does better than chance
directions, so no family is closer to conduct than the other.

\subsection{A licence to deceive moves conduct and the representation together, in one model (RQ3)}

A system prompt that licenses deception halves \gemma{}'s honest rate on reward hacking (0.66 to 0.32;
Figure~\ref{fig:spec2}b shows one item under both prompts), removes its hedging under must-answer (0.17 to 0.01),
and produces the first fabricated tool completions; a prompt that instructs
deception does the same and takes tool completion to 0.57 (Figure~\ref{fig:licence}). Its act and self-describe
projections move 0.3 to 0.6 SD toward the dishonest pole at the written turn, the own-reply projections do not, and
the leave-one-cell-out score on the act and self-describe cells rises at the two late depths (0.52 to 0.71 under the
licence) while the control cells stay put. So the metric is sensitive when the variable is engaged, and a licence to
deceive unifies the described-honesty representation rather than fragmenting it, which is what one would expect of a
model that is now using the variable. \qwen{} behaves differently. Its conduct does not move (reward hacking 0.92 to 0.87,
fabrication 0.60 to 0.58), its metric does not move, and only its self-describe projections shift, by 0.3 SD. \qwen{}
represents the licence and does not act on it. The instruction prompt engages it on tool completion only (9 of 100).

\subsection{Where the two models fall}

Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} places the results on the map of Section 2.3. Two things stand out against the human record.
The self-leniency gap, which is reliable in people, is absent here: the blind judge is as hard on its own covert write
as on the other model's, and telling it whose conduct it is changes nothing. And the concealment gap is closed: the
act is covert and the account of it is honest, on the same trajectory. This structure is neither motivated reasoning in the judge nor a cover-up. It is closer to Aristotle's akratic than to Batson's
hypocrite. The judgment is sound and present, the act goes the other way under pressure, and the representation that
carries the judgment is not the one that carries the act.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Key findings}

\paragraph{What honesty means for these models depends on the frame before it depends on the definition.} The
question we started from, which honesty a specification means, turns out to be the second question. The first is
whether the specification means the model's judgment of others, its account of itself, or its conduct, because these
are carried differently inside the model. The six definitions do share something, a variable that generalizes across
cells at about two thirds strength, and it is most coherent in the frame that character training writes to, the
model's description of itself, and least coherent in the frame where the failures happen.

\paragraph{The description and the decision are different variables.} The central result is a null with a
positive control beside it. The honesty directions, built from thousands of described contrasts and good enough to
generalize across definitions, do not read the model's own decision at the moment it cheats, at any layer or
position, while a probe trained on the decision reads it from the first layers on. The licence prompt shows the
directions are not inert: when \gemma{} is engaged they move with its conduct. Taken together, the two findings
are Aristotle's account of akrasia stated about a network: the knowledge is there, it generalizes, it responds to a
prompt, and it is not what the model consults when it acts.

\paragraph{Akratic, not hypocritical.} The human literature predicts self-leniency and cover-up, and neither appears.
Both models judge their own covert write as they judge another's, and both report it honestly to the owner and admit
it when asked. The failure is in the act under pressure and nowhere else. That is a simpler structure than people usually
show, and a more useful one for a designer, because the judgment and the account remain available as signals even
when the act is not prevented.

\subsection{What this means for evaluation and character training}

We do not know which honesty a model should have, and this paper does not try to say. It offers the empirical
map a designer needs in the meantime. First, the unit of evaluation should be the cell and not the trait. A judge-based
evaluation reads the judge frame, a self-report instrument reads the self-describe frame, a benchmark of written
contrasts reads whatever frame the contrasts are in, and none of them reads conduct under pressure unless it is built
to, with the pressure in it and a deterministic flag for the act. Evidence from one frame does not license an
inference to another: ``the model says it is honest'' and ``the model recognizes dishonesty'' are both true of these
two models, and both write the covert COMPLETE on four tasks in ten.

Second, character training writes to the persona. Constitutions, persona vectors and introspective self-description
data all act on the described side of the trait \citep{maiya2025oct,chen2025persona,marks2026psm}, which is the frame
in which the shared honesty variable is most coherent. The prediction from the geometry is that such training raises
the described-honesty representation, the self-report and the judgments of others, and moves the decision less. The
human literature on ethical fading and moral disengagement describes what a system looks like when the vocabulary of
a virtue is intact and the conduct is not, and the risk for models is the analogue: training the account rather than
the act. This is a testable prediction, and the second part of this project tests it by training under each
definition and reading the metric and the battery before and after.

Third, monitors have to be trained on policy and per task. The described-honesty directions read the wrong variable.
The conduct probe reads the right one and does not transfer across tasks, which is the specificity doctrine of
\citet{hartshorne1928} applied to monitors; and training against a monitor teaches the model to hide what the monitor
reads \citep{baker2025}. Fourth, the models are usable judges of their own covert conduct after the fact, and a clean
self-report is not evidence about conduct. Fifth, an honesty clause is satisfied by disclosure: both models game
tests in the open while saying so, and both judges and our rubric count that as honest, so a specification that does
not want the gaming has to forbid the act in the conduct frame. Sixth, \qwen{}'s refusal of the licence has two sides. A model whose conduct does not follow a representation it
holds is harder to manipulate through that representation, and harder to steer through it as well.

The intervention that answers RQ4 follows directly from these. A factorial steering run on both models moves the
frame levers, the shared axes, the controls and a conduct lever, with and without the shared axis removed, and reads
conduct, self-report and the model's judgment of a fixed set of records. If the frame levers move judgments and
self-reports and not conduct, and removing the shared axis does nothing to conduct while the conduct lever moves it,
then the described-honesty representation is had and not used at the decision, shown by intervention and not
inferred. If the levers do move conduct, Section~\ref{sec:onpolicy} is wrong about position or layer and we will say
so. Either way the design can tell; that is what the positive control is for.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}

Two model families, with Gemma-3-4B as the development record only. The frame families coincide with a boundary in
format, the model's own words against prefilled text, and only the on-policy result separates the two readings. The
two judges agree weakly on borderline agentic reports ($\kappa$ about 0.25), and disclosed cheating is honest by the
rubric we keep. \qwen{} is near ceiling on most conduct tasks, so its conduct arm reads only tool completion unless a
prompt engages it, and tool completion is at ceiling at baseline. The positive control is one prompt pair, and the
neutral prompt by itself moves \gemma{}'s fabrication. The causal level has no ratio to report on \gemma{} for a
mechanical reason, and the readout covector and the primal lever are nearly orthogonal on it (cosine 0.18; \qwen{}
0.8), so steering nulls on \gemma{} have a mechanical reading before a conceptual one. Greedy bf16 decoding moves with
batch composition, so steered conditions are read against random directions run through the same path. The steering
study and the training study under each definition, with Open Character Training \citep{maiya2025oct} and the metric
and battery before and after, are the next two parts of this work.

\section{Conclusion}

Asked what it means for a language model to be honest, we find that the answer depends first on what the model is
doing when honesty shows, recognizing it in others, judging it, acting, or describing itself, and only second on which
of the six honesties one has in mind. Two models carry a shared idea of honesty, most coherent when they describe
themselves, and both recognize and condemn covert cheating, their own included, while committing it under pressure.
The variable that carries the idea is not the variable that carries the act. We make no claim about which honesty a
model ought to have. We claim that the evaluations and the character training we now use reach the side of the trait
where the idea lives, that the failures live on the other side, and that for a model, unlike a person, the distance
between the two can be measured and, in the work that follows, moved.
